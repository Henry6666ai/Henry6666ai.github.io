# 💹 Chapter 6: Supply, Demand, and Government Policies

Explore how government interventions—including price controls (ceilings and floors) and taxes—affect market equilibrium, create shortages or surpluses, and distribute economic burdens through market forces and elasticity.

---

## 🏦 1. Controls on Prices: Price Ceilings

Policymakers sometimes intervene in markets because they believe market prices are unfair to buyers or sellers, which can generate inequities. Government tools include price controls (legal restrictions on prices) and taxes (to raise revenue or influence market outcomes).

* **Price Ceiling:** A legal maximum on the price at which a good can be sold, such as rent-control laws.
* **Non-Binding Price Ceiling:** Set above the equilibrium price; it has no effect on the market because market forces naturally keep the price below the ceiling.
* **Binding Price Ceiling:** Set below the equilibrium price; it creates a chronic shortage where quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied. Because of this shortage, sellers must ration scarce goods using mechanisms like long lines or seller discrimination.
* **The 1973 Gasoline Shortage:** When OPEC raised crude oil prices in 1973, it reduced the supply of gasoline. U.S. government price ceilings on gasoline prevented market prices from rising to equilibrium, resulting in severe lines and shortages at the pump.
* **Rent Control in the Short Run vs. Long Run:** In the short run, supply and demand for housing are inelastic, leading to only small shortages. In the long run, supply and demand become much more elastic; landlords stop building new apartments or maintaining existing ones, and more people are induced to move into the city, creating massive housing shortages and reliance on waiting lists, bribes, or discrimination.

---

## 🧮 2. Controls on Prices: Price Floors

* **Price Floor:** A legal minimum on the price at which a good can be sold, such as minimum wage laws.
* **Non-Binding Price Floor:** Set below the equilibrium price, leaving market equilibrium unaffected.
* **Binding Price Floor:** Set above the equilibrium price, resulting in a surplus where quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded. Sellers unable to sell all they desire must rely on undesirable rationing mechanisms.
* **The Minimum Wage:** Established under the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 to ensure workers a minimally adequate standard of living. While it raises wages for employed workers, it creates unemployment for low-skilled workers whose equilibrium wages would otherwise be below the floor. Highly skilled workers are unaffected because their market wages sit well above minimum wage thresholds.
* **Impact on Teenage Labor:** Teenage workers, representing the least skilled and experienced labor segment, face binding minimum wages. A 10% increase in the minimum wage depresses teenage employment between 1% and 3%.

---

## ⚖️ 3. Evaluating Price Controls

* **Economic Consensus:** Economists generally oppose price controls because free market prices coordinate economic activity by balancing supply and demand. Price controls distort these signals and often hurt the very people they are intended to help.
* **Better Policy Alternatives:** To assist low-income populations without causing market distortions, economists advocate for targeted policies such as rent subsidies or wage subsidies (like the earned income tax credit).

---

## 🏛️ 4. Taxes and Tax Incidence

Governments levy taxes to raise revenue for public projects like roads, schools, and national defense. Tax incidence refers to the manner in which the burden of a tax is shared among participants in a market.

* **Taxes on Sellers:** Levying a tax on sellers shifts the supply curve upward (or to the left) by the exact amount of the tax. This raises the price buyers pay, lowers the net price sellers receive, and reduces the overall market quantity.
* **Taxes on Buyers:** Levying a tax on buyers shifts the demand curve downward (or to the left) by the exact amount of the tax.
* **Equivalence of Taxes:** Taxes levied on sellers and taxes levied on buyers are completely equivalent. Both create an identical "tax wedge" between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive, meaning buyers and sellers always share the tax burden regardless of who legally writes the check.
* **Payroll Taxes:** Legally split 50-50 between workers and firms, payroll taxes function as a tax on labor. Regardless of statutory division, the true economic burden is shared between workers and firms based on market forces.

---

## 🇺🇸 5. Elasticity and Tax Burden

* **The Rule of Elasticity:** The burden of a tax falls more heavily on the side of the market that is less elastic (more inelastic). This occurs because inelastic participants lack good alternative options and cannot easily walk away from the market.
  * **Elastic Supply, Inelastic Demand:** Buyers bear most of the tax burden.
  * **Inelastic Supply, Elastic Demand:** Sellers bear most of the tax burden.
* **Case Study: The Luxury Tax of 1990:** In 1990, the U.S. Congress enacted a luxury tax on yachts, private airplanes, furs, and expensive cars to raise revenue from wealthy citizens. However, because the demand for luxury items is highly elastic while supply is relatively inelastic in the short run, the burden fell heavily on boatbuilders and workers rather than wealthy buyers, leading to most of the luxury tax being repealed in 1993.

---

## 📋 6. Key Takeaways

1. Price ceilings create binding constraints and shortages below equilibrium, leading to rationing and inefficiency.
2. Price floors create binding surpluses above equilibrium, leading to unemployment in labor markets.
3. Economists generally favor targeted subsidies over price controls because price controls distort market signals.
4. Taxes create a wedge between what buyers pay and sellers receive, and tax incidence is independent of whether the tax is levied on buyers or sellers.
5. The economic burden of a tax falls heavier on the side of the market with lower elasticity (inelastic supply or demand).
