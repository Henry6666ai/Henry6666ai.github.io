            <section id="introduction-to-economics" class="content-section">
                <h2>Introduction to Economics</h2>
                <p>
                    The word "economics" originates from the Greek word <em>oikonomos</em>, which translates to "one who manages a household". Households and economies share a great deal in common because both must make numerous decisions regarding the allocation of scarce resources.
                </p>
                <ul>
                    <li>Society faces many decisions on how to allocate resources and output.</li>
                    <li>Resources are scarce, meaning society has a limited nature of resources and cannot produce all the goods and services people wish to have.</li>
                    <li><strong>Economics</strong> is formally defined as the study of how society manages its scarce resources.</li>
                </ul>
                <p>Economists study several core areas:</p>
                <ul>
                    <li>How people make decisions, including how they work, buy, save, and invest.</li>
                    <li>How people interact with one another.</li>
                    <li>The forces and trends that affect the economy as a whole, such as growth in average income, the fraction of the population that cannot find work, and the rate at which prices are rising.</li>
                </ul>
            </section>

            <section id="how-people-make-decisions" class="content-section">
                <h2>How People Make Decisions</h2>
                <p class="section-lead">Decision-making by individuals is governed by the first four principles of economics.</p>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 1: People Face Trade-offs</h3>
                    <p>There is no such thing as a free lunch. To get something we like, we typically have to give up something else we also like.</p>
                    <ul>
                        <li>Making decisions requires trading off one goal against another, such as giving up an hour of television to study for one more hour.</li>
                        <li>Society faces major trade-offs, including national defense versus consumer goods ("guns and butter"), a clean environment versus a high level of income, and efficiency versus equality.</li>
                        <li><strong>Efficiency</strong> refers to getting the maximum benefits from scarce resources (represented by the size of the economic pie).</li>
                        <li><strong>Equality</strong> refers to distributing economic prosperity uniformly among the members of society (represented by how the pie is divided into individual slices).</li>
                        <li>Public policies like welfare systems, unemployment insurance, and individual income taxes aim to achieve greater equality but often reduce efficiency.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 2: The Cost of Something Is What You Give Up to Get It</h3>
                    <p>Because people face trade-offs, making decisions requires comparing the costs and benefits of alternative courses of action.</p>
                    <ul>
                        <li><strong>Opportunity cost</strong> is defined as whatever must be given up to obtain some item.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 3: Rational People Think at the Margin</h3>
                    <ul>
                        <li><strong>Rational people</strong> systematically and purposefully do the best they can to achieve their objectives given the available opportunities.</li>
                        <li><strong>Marginal changes</strong> are small incremental adjustments to a plan of action.</li>
                        <li>Rational decision makers make choices by comparing marginal benefits and marginal costs, taking action only if the marginal benefit exceeds the marginal cost.</li>
                        <li>This marginal framework explains puzzles like why water is cheap while diamonds are expensive: a person's willingness to pay is based on the marginal benefit of an extra unit, which depends on how many units they already possess.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 4: People Respond to Incentives</h3>
                    <ul>
                        <li>An <strong>incentive</strong> is something that induces a person to act, such as the prospect of a punishment or reward.</li>
                        <li>Higher prices induce buyers to consume less and sellers to produce more.</li>
                        <li>Public policies change costs or benefits, thereby altering people's behavior, though they can sometimes have unintended consequences. For example, seat belt laws reduce the injury costs of accidents, which can lead people to drive faster and less carefully, resulting in little change in driver deaths and an increase in pedestrian deaths.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>
            </section>

            <section id="how-people-interact" class="content-section">
                <h2>How People Interact</h2>
                <p class="section-lead">How individuals and firms interact with one another is governed by principles 5 through 7.</p>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 5: Trade Can Make Everyone Better Off</h3>
                    <ul>
                        <li>Trade allows each person to specialize in the activities they do best.</li>
                        <li>Through trade, people can enjoy a greater variety of goods and services at a lower cost.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 6: Markets Are Usually a Good Way to Organize Economic Activity</h3>
                    <ul>
                        <li>In a centrally planned economy, government officials (central planners) decide what goods and services are produced, how much is produced, and who produces and consumes them.</li>
                        <li>In a <strong>market economy</strong>, resources are allocated through the decentralized decisions of many firms and households interacting in markets, guided by prices and self-interest.</li>
                        <li>Adam Smith introduced the concept of the <strong>"invisible hand"</strong>, which describes how households and firms interacting in markets act as if guided by an invisible hand to lead them to desirable market outcomes.</li>
                        <li>Modern innovations like Uber demonstrate the market mechanism in action: surge pricing increases the supply of car services when demand is highest and allocates them to consumers who value them most.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 7: Governments Can Sometimes Improve Market Outcomes</h3>
                    <p>While markets are usually successful, governments are needed to enforce rules and maintain institutions (like property rights) that are key to a market economy.</p>
                    <ul>
                        <li><strong>Property rights</strong> refer to the ability of an individual to own and exercise control over scarce resources.</li>
                        <li>Governments intervene to promote efficiency and avoid <strong>market failure</strong>, which occurs when a market left on its own fails to allocate resources efficiently. Two main causes of market failure are <strong>externalities</strong> (the impact of one person's actions on the well-being of a bystander, such as pollution) and <strong>market power</strong> (the ability of a single economic actor to substantially influence market prices).</li>
                        <li>Governments also intervene to promote equality and address disparities in economic well-being through public policies.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>
            </section>

            <section id="how-the-economy-as-a-whole-works" class="content-section">
                <h2>The Economy as a Whole</h2>
                <p class="section-lead">Macroeconomic forces and economy-wide mechanisms are explained by principles 8 through 10.</p>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 8: A Country’s Standard of Living Depends on Its Ability to Produce Goods and Services</h3>
                    <ul>
                        <li>There are massive differences in living standards across countries and over time, driven almost entirely by <strong>productivity</strong>.</li>
                        <li><strong>Productivity</strong> is the quantity of goods and services produced from each unit of labor input. A nation's productivity growth determines the growth rate of its average income.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 9: Prices Rise When the Government Prints Too Much Money</h3>
                    <ul>
                        <li><strong>Inflation</strong> is an increase in the overall level of prices in the economy.</li>
                        <li>When a government creates large quantities of money, the value of money falls, causing prices to rise persistently.</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>

                <div class="principle-card">
                    <h3>Principle 10: Society Faces a Short-Run Trade-off between Inflation and Unemployment</h3>
                    <ul>
                        <li>In the short run (a period of a year or two), economic policies that inject money into the economy stimulate overall spending and demand.</li>
                        <li>Firms respond by raising prices, hiring more workers, and producing more goods and services, which temporarily lowers unemployment.</li>
                        <li>This creates a short-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment, playing a key role in the analysis of the <strong>business cycle</strong> (fluctuations in economic activity like employment and production).</li>
                    </ul>
                </div>
            </section>

            <section id="key-takeaways" class="content-section">
                <h2>Key Takeaways</h2>
                <ol class="takeaways-list">
                    <li><strong>Economics is scarcity management:</strong> Economics is the study of how society manages its scarce resources in the face of unlimited wants.</li>
                    <li><strong>Microeconomic decision rules:</strong> Individuals make decisions by weighing trade-offs, considering opportunity costs, thinking at the margin, and responding to incentives.</li>
                    <li><strong>Market coordination:</strong> Trade benefits everyone through specialization, and market economies use the "invisible hand" of prices to coordinate activity efficiently.</li>
                    <li><strong>Government role:</strong> Governments can improve market outcomes when market failures (such as externalities or market power) occur or when property rights need enforcement.</li>
                    <li><strong>Macroeconomic realities:</strong> Living standards depend heavily on productivity, inflation is driven by excessive money printing, and a short-run trade-off exists between inflation and unemployment.</li>
                </ol>
            </section>
