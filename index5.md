# 💹 Chapter 5: Elasticity and Its Application

This lecture examines the concept of elasticity, exploring how buyers and sellers respond to changes in price, income, and market conditions across various economic applications.

---

## 🏦 1. The Elasticity of Demand

Elasticity is a general measure of the responsiveness of quantity demanded or quantity supplied to a change in one of its determinants. The price elasticity of demand specifically measures how much the quantity demanded of a good responds to a change in the price of that good.

### Elastic versus Inelastic Demand

- **Elastic demand**: The quantity demanded responds substantially to changes in price.
- **Inelastic demand**: The quantity demanded responds only slightly to changes in price.

### Determinants of Price Elasticity of Demand

- **Availability of close substitutes**: Goods with close substitutes tend to have more elastic demand.
- **Necessities versus luxuries**: Necessities generally have inelastic demand, whereas luxuries have elastic demand.
- **Definition of the market**: Narrowly defined markets have more elastic demand because it is easier to find substitutes for specific items.
- **Time horizon**: Demand becomes more elastic over longer time horizons as consumers adjust their habits or find alternatives.

### Computing Price Elasticity and Demand Curve Varieties

Price elasticity of demand is computed as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price, using absolute values (dropping any minus signs) and the midpoint method:

$$\text{Price Elasticity of Demand} = \left| \frac{\% \Delta Q^d}{\% \Delta P} \right|$$

- **Elastic**: Price elasticity of demand is greater than 1.
- **Inelastic**: Price elasticity of demand is less than 1.
- **Unit Elastic**: Price elasticity of demand equals 1.
- **Perfectly Inelastic**: Price elasticity of demand equals 0, represented visually by a vertical demand curve.
- **Perfectly Elastic**: Price elasticity of demand equals infinity, represented visually by a horizontal demand curve.

*Note: The flatter the demand curve passes through a given point, the greater the price elasticity of demand.*

---

## 🧮 2. Total Revenue and Elasticity

Total revenue ($TR$) is the amount paid by buyers and received by sellers of a good, calculated as the price times the quantity sold:

$$TR = P \times Q$$

### How Price Changes Affect Total Revenue

- **When demand is inelastic (elasticity < 1)**: Price and total revenue move in the same direction; if price rises, total revenue increases.
- **When demand is elastic (elasticity > 1)**: Price and total revenue move in opposite directions; if price rises, total revenue decreases.
- **When demand is unit elastic (elasticity = 1)**: Total revenue remains constant when the price changes.

### Elasticity along a Linear Demand Curve

Although the slope of a linear demand curve is constant ($\text{rise} / \text{run}$), its price elasticity is not. At points with a low price and high quantity, the demand curve is inelastic. At points with a high price and low quantity, the demand curve is elastic.

---

## ⚖️ 3. Other Demand Elasticities

### Income Elasticity of Demand

Measures how much the quantity demanded of a good responds to a change in consumers' income, calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in income:

$$\text{Income Elasticity of Demand} = \frac{\% \Delta Q^d}{\% \Delta \text{Income}}$$

**Normal goods** have positive income elasticity (smaller for necessities, larger for luxuries), while **inferior goods** have negative income elasticity.

### Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand

Measures how much the quantity demanded of one good responds to a change in the price of another good, calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded of the first good divided by the percentage change in price of the second good:

$$\text{Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand} = \frac{\% \Delta Q_1^d}{\% \Delta P_2}$$

**Substitutes** have a positive cross-price elasticity, while **complements** have a negative cross-price elasticity.

---

## 🏛️ 4. The Elasticity of Supply

The price elasticity of supply measures how much the quantity supplied of a good responds to a change in its price, calculated as the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price:

$$\text{Price Elasticity of Supply} = \frac{\% \Delta Q^s}{\% \Delta P}$$

It depends heavily on the flexibility of sellers to change the amount of the good they produce.

### Elastic versus Inelastic Supply

- **Elastic supply**: Quantity supplied responds substantially to changes in price.
- **Inelastic supply**: Quantity supplied responds only slightly to changes in price.
- **Time Period Determinant**: Supply is much more elastic in the long run than in the short run because firms can build new plants or enter/exit markets over time.

### Computing and Categorizing Supply Curves

Supply elasticity is always positive and calculated using the midpoint method. Supply curves can be unit elastic ($=1$), elastic ($>1$), inelastic ($<1$), perfectly inelastic ($=0$, vertical curve), or perfectly elastic ($=\infty$, horizontal curve). Due to production capacity limits, supply elasticity can vary along a single curve, being high at low quantities and low at high quantities.

---

## 🇺🇸 5. Applications of Elasticity

### 1. Can Good News for Farming Be Bad News for Farmers?
An agricultural technological advance (like a new wheat hybrid) increases per-acre production, shifting the supply curve to the right. Because the demand for basic food items is inelastic, the resulting drop in price causes total revenue to fall for farmers as a whole, illustrating why public policy sometimes induces farmers not to plant crops.

### 2. Why Did OPEC Fail to Keep the Price of Oil High?
In the short run, oil supply and demand are relatively inelastic, so a reduction in supply causes a large price spike. In the long run, supply and demand are more elastic, meaning the same shift in supply causes a much smaller increase in price.

### 3. Does Drug Interdiction Increase or Decrease Drug-related Crime?
Drug interdiction reduces the supply of illegal drugs (shifts supply left). Because the demand for addictive drugs is inelastic, this policy raises the price and total revenue, which can inadvertently increase drug-related crime. By contrast, drug education reduces the demand for drugs (shifts demand left), lowering both price, quantity, and overall crime.

---

## 📋 6. Key Takeaways

- **Price elasticity of demand** measures consumer responsiveness to price changes and is computed using the midpoint method.
- **Total revenue** rises with price increases when demand is inelastic, but falls when demand is elastic.
- **Income and cross-price elasticities** help classify goods as normal, inferior, substitutes, or complements.
- **Price elasticity of supply** measures producer responsiveness and is generally higher in the long run.
- **Applying elasticity** helps explain real-world economic paradoxes, such as agricultural technology impacts, oil price volatility, and the outcomes of drug policies.
